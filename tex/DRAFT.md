The earliest experiments that now anchor the history of virology showed that the transmissible agents of tobacco mosaic disease and of foot-and-mouth disease passed through filters that retained bacteria (Iwanowski 1892; Beijerinck 1898; Loeffler & Frosch 1898). These "filterable agents" could be neither seen nor cultivated by the bacteriological methods then available, and for the following half-century the presence of such an agent was inferred indirectly, from what followed inoculation of a susceptible plant, animal, or embryonated egg.

That situation changed with the introduction of cell culture. Enders, Weller, and Robbins described in 1949 the propagation of poliovirus in non-neural human tissue cultures (Enders et al. 1949), work that was recognized with the 1954 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, and they shortly afterward described degeneration of the inoculated cell sheet in such cultures (Robbins et al. 1950). This degeneration—the cytopathic effect (CPE)—was thereafter adopted as the routine marker of viral presence in the cell-culture system: after inoculation, morphological change of the monolayer came to be read as an indication that the inoculum contained a virus.

The cytopathic endpoint did more than make an agent detectable; it specified the culture—the isolate—from which that agent could then be physically characterized. Some of this characterization antedates cell culture, but only for agents obtainable in bulk outside tissue: tobacco mosaic virus was crystallized in 1935 (Stanley 1935), its particle mass was measured by analytical ultracentrifugation over the following years (Eriksson-Quensel & Svedberg 1936), and it was among the first viruses imaged in the electron microscope (Kausche, Pfankuch & Ruska 1939). For the human and animal viruses, no bulk route of that kind existed; their study came to depend on cell culture, in which the CPE endpoint both announces the agent's presence and designates the culture from which purified material is drawn. Genome sequencing followed the same dependence: the first complete genome was read from the RNA bacteriophage MS2 grown in bacteria (Fiers et al. 1976), and the first complete genome of a eukaryotic virus, the polyomavirus SV40, was read from virus grown in cell culture (Fiers et al. 1978).

Cell-culture virus isolation has since been standardized and written into institutional protocols. This study begins from a specific feature of those protocols: at the inoculation step, two independent variables of the culture system are changed at once, while a single dependent variable is read. The monolayer is switched from growth medium containing approximately 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) to maintenance medium containing approximately 2% FBS, and is then monitored for CPE as the indicator of viral presence. We refer to this as the two-independent-variables, one-dependent-variable (2I1D) pattern: inoculum and serum concentration are co-varied, while CPE is observed.

Five questions follow, which the remainder of the paper addresses in turn: (i) does published virus-isolation practice generally follow the 2I1D pattern; (ii) what purpose is stated for the serum reduction at inoculation; (iii) is the control that would separate the two variables—an uninoculated culture carried through the same maintenance and serum step—performed and reported; (iv) can CPE-classifiable morphology be produced in uninoculated cultures by serum reduction alone; and (v) if CPE does not uniquely indicate the presence of a virus, in what ways might the characterizations derived from the CPE-defined isolate—particle properties and genome—be flawed, and might the detection methods later built from those characterizations be flawed in consequence?

That situation changed with the introduction of cell culture. Enders, Weller, and Robbins described in 1949 the propagation of poliovirus in non-neural human tissue cultures (Enders et al. 1949), work that was recognized with the 1954 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, and they shortly afterward described degeneration of the inoculated cell sheet in such cultures (Robbins et al. 1950). Cell culture was not the first route to a virus, and the speed with which it displaced the earlier routes is itself informative. Bulk purification had already shown that a virus can be a discrete, uniform particle—tobacco mosaic virus was crystallized in 1935 (Stanley 1935), and further plant viruses and bacteriophages were obtained in quantity over the following decade—but bulk purification is a preparative endpoint, not a detector: it requires an agent growable to grams and already homogeneous, and it can find nothing in a diluted or contaminated sample. The embryonated egg (Goodpasture et al. 1931) grew a few animal viruses, such as influenza and vaccinia, in preparable amount, but only those that replicate in the chick embryo, and it offered no convenient visible readout with which to follow an agent. Cell culture supplied what both lacked at once. Permissive cell lines grew a wide range of human and animal viruses, including many with no egg or animal host; the monolayer offered a visible—and, once plaques were introduced, quantitatively countable—signal of the agent's presence (Dulbecco 1952); and the infected cells yielded the agent in preparable quantity. This combination is why the method overtook its predecessors so quickly, and why the physical characterization of human and animal viruses—electron microscopy, gradient centrifugation, genome sequencing, and the first animal-virus crystal, poliovirus, in 1955 (Schaffer & Schwerdt 1955)—became routine only after cell culture.

Cell-culture virus isolation has since been standardized and written into institutional protocols. The monolayer is switched from growth medium containing approximately 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) to maintenance medium containing approximately 2% FBS, and is then monitored for CPE as the indicator of viral presence. We refer to this as the two-independent-variables, one-dependent-variable (2I1D) pattern: inoculum and serum concentration are co-varied, while CPE is observed.

The pivotal role of cell culture in virology thus turned on a single, convenient endpoint—the morphological read conducted after inoculation—which makes the specificity of that endpoint worth examining. Given the above, it is reasonable to state that in cell culture experiments where a virus is being “isolated,” there are two independent variables being manipulated and one dependent variable being observed:
	- Independent variables:
		- Virus added or not
		- FBS concentration
	- Dependent variable:
		- CPE in cell culture

Literature on virus isolation aligns with these guidelines [1-8], often employing reduced FBS during propagation and relying on CPE for detection. For example [1,3,4]:
	- In Ge et al. (2013), Vero E6 cells were maintained in DMEM with 10% FCS, but virus propagation used DMEM with 2% FCS. CPE was observed daily to detect viral presence.
	- In Zhou et al. (2020), Vero E6 cells were cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS, with clear CPE observed after three days of incubation as a key indicator of viral infection.
	- In Zhao et al. (2019), methods involved virus isolation in cell cultures with CPE as a general indicator, consistent with standard practices.

FBS concentration could act as a confounder in inducing CPE in cell cultures, potentially making CPE non-specific to the presence of virus particles. Controlled comparisons are needed before treating CPE under dual-media Isolation conditions as virus-specific.



The control experiment offers preliminary evidence: Reduced FBS alone induced CPE-like changes without virus, aligning with literature but exposing a media control gap. Public data enables re-analysis for quantification.

If CPE under dual-media Isolation conditions is not virus-specific, Isolation-dependent culture endpoints inherit that limitation. This draft restricts itself to the culture confound and related practice evidence; see also `tex/FRAMING_LIMITATIONS_ANONYMITY.md`.

## R1 Isolation practice corpus

*Empirical description of dual-serum Isolation practice and institutional recipes. This section describes how Isolation is commonly done; claims are limited to what the cited corpus documents about growth/maintenance serum practice.*

### Methods (corpus construction)

We assembled two related reference sets for capital-I **Isolation** (culture → cytopathic readout → passage / Isolate as an operational product):

1. **Practice papers (dual-FBS corpus).** Peer-reviewed Isolation or primary diagnostic isolation Methods that state **both** a numeric pre-inoculation (growth) serum percentage and a numeric post-inoculation (maintenance / virus culture) serum percentage (including explicit 0%). Rows were coded only when both values were explicit in the source; percentages were not invented from context alone. Any dual numeric pair was retained with **equal weight**—including the common 10%→2% pattern and less common equal-holds, increases, or near-serum-free maintenance—without preferential sampling or apology for modal recipes. Sources include open-access full texts and extracted Methods quotes; the working table is `r1_isolation_standards_and_practice/isolation-refs.csv` (189 rows; **169** plotted dual-FBS rows).

2. **Institutional guidelines / SOPs (overlay).** Separate from the practice-paper corpus, we recorded published institutional recipes that prescribe growth versus maintenance serum for diagnostic Isolation (e.g., ATCC Virology Culture Guide, CLSI M41-A, ASM CPE protocols, NEADL/CIRAD PPRV SOP, WOAH Terrestrial Manual chapter material, WHO EPI, CDC measles lab tools, Health Canada / CCDR B95-a measles support). Plot overlays use **n = 8** guidelines with parseable dual percentages.

**Exclusions from plots.** USDA APHIS VIRPRO1013 (9 CFR §§ 113.55/113.46) is retained in the institutional reference table for completeness but **excluded from distribution plots**: it addresses master-seed / extraneous-agent safety testing rather than diagnostic Isolation practice.

Distribution figures were regenerated with `r1_isolation_standards_and_practice/plot_fbs_distributions.py` into `r1_isolation_standards_and_practice/fbs_distribution_plots/`. The plotter successfully parsed **n = 169** practice rows (one file row lacked a soft-numeric parse, as in prior passes).

**Pattern mix (descriptive only).** Pre-inoculation values are dominated by 10% (139/169), with smaller counts at 5%, 8%, 7.5%, 15%, and occasional low or zero serum. Post-inoculation values are dominated by 2% (113/169), with a long tail including 0%, 1%, 5%, 10%, and other maintenance levels. The corpus therefore shows a **modal** growth→maintenance step-down consistent with institutional recipes, together with a minority of other dual combinations. These frequencies describe practice as sampled; they are not evidence that any single recipe is uniquely correct, and they are not a cherry-picked contrast set.

**Scope.** This corpus is a **representative practice description** of dual-serum Isolation Methods wording. It does not by itself establish CPE virus-specificity, and it does not substitute for controlled experiments that vary serum without inoculum. Claims about control quality and medium confounds are scored separately.

## methods

### experiment
To explore this hypothesis, a control experiment was commissioned through a certified U.S.-based CRO using Vero E6 cells (ATCC-CRL-1586), with no virus added. Two identical paths differed only in FBS: Path A at 10%, Path B at 2%. After preparation (thawing and passaging) and experimental stages (daily imaging), greater CPE-like changes appeared in Path B, shown in over 50 light microscope images (EVOS FL at 10x/20x) and 170 TEM images (Tecnai G2 Spirit BioTWIN). Raw data is on Zenodo [9] for analysis and replication.

### Identification of standard CPE descriptors
\subsection{Standard CPE descriptors}
Characteristic CPE descriptors were extracted from Table 7 of the CLSI M41 Guideline \cite{clsi} through an iterative review process. First, the full table was examined to identify a set of ten descriptors: rounded, ballooned/enlarged, syncytia, vacuolation, detachment, granularity, refractile, cytoplasmic strands, nonspecific degeneration, and no CPE. An incidence table was then constructed by identifying every instance of these terms (or close semantic correlates) in text entries of the ``Appearance'' column, see Table 1 below.

\input{clsi-table7-descriptors.tex}

Next, the same mapping procedure was applied to the CRO image descriptions to generate a second incidence table (CLSI descriptor set + image descriptions). Only five of the original ten CLSI descriptors appeared in the CRO descriptions. A sixth term, ``Dying Cells'', emerged directly from the CRO text and was retained as a distinct category (Dy) rather than being forced into the CLSI ``nonspecific degeneration'' bin. The resulting \textit{CPE descriptor set} used for ground truth and all subsequent analyses therefore consisted of: Dying cells (Dy), Rounded (Ro), Vacuolation (V), Detached (D), Granularity (G), and Refractile (Re).
