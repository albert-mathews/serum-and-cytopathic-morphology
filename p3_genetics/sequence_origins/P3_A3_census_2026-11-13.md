# P3 A3 — same-stock re-sequencing census

**Date:** 2026-11-13
**Status:** Systematic scoring pass over the Step 1 O/C pairs + new literature cases.
**Question:** across named viral stocks/lineages that were re-sequenced, how often do the assemblies *diverge*
(correction, parallel length/content differences, passage drift, intrastrain variation, retraction, size
correction) versus *reproduce cleanly*?

## Method

- Denominator: the Step 1 origin/confirmation corpus plus new literature cases, restricted to pairs that are a
  **re-sequencing of the same stock / lineage** (a C event re-sequencing the material of an O event, multiple
  parallel O events of one stock, or a named-stock correction). New-field-isolate pairs (a different patient /
  outbreak / serotype) are excluded — they are not a re-sequencing of the same stock.
- Each pair is scored `divergent` (a documented correction / parallel divergence / passage drift / intrastrain
  variation / retraction / size correction), `unclear` (re-sequenced but divergence not stated), or `clean`
  (re-sequenced with no divergence noted).

## Reading

Across every same-stock / same-lineage re-sequencing pair we could assemble from the Step 1 corpus and the
literature, **none reproduces cleanly**: 18 of 20 document divergence (correction, parallel length/content
differences, passage drift, intrastrain variation, retraction, or size correction) and 2 are unresolved (the
re-sequencing is noted but no explicit divergence comparison is stated). The cleanest, least-arguable subset is
the **correction** class — 8 named reference genomes the field itself had to fix after deposit (AD169 929 bp,
AD169 UL15A, HPV16, Ad17, adenovirus Geck ORF, AAV2, HVT frameshift, plus the measles retraction).

This is direct CNJ150 evidence at the culture+CPE layer (see A3-001 … A3-018):
the deposited "complete genome" of a culture-derived type material is repeatedly shown to be **wrong or
unstable**, and the instability is *published* — i.e. detectable, not papered over (CNJ151 is thereby rejected, and
the finding sits under CNJ150).

**Honest scope guard — publication (ascertainment) bias.** The census is a literature *retrospective*, and the
literature is a biased record:

1. **Clean confirmations are unpublishable.** If a lab re-sequences a named stock and reproduces the published
   reference exactly, there is nothing novel to report — journals do not print "we re-sequenced X and it matched."
   So the set of "re-sequencings that reproduced cleanly" is systematically *invisible* in the literature, no
   matter how often it actually happens in reality.

2. **Divergences are the only publishable outcome.** Finding that a deposited genome differs, is wrong, or must be
   retracted *is* newsworthy — it corrects prior work — so corrections, errata, and "the published sequence was
   found to differ" notes are exactly the events that get written up. The literature is therefore pre-loaded with
   divergences and stripped of clean confirmations.

3. **So 18/20 divergent / 0 clean does NOT estimate the true divergence rate.** It is a lower bound on the
   *record* — a statement that divergence demonstrably happens, in multiple differently-caused forms (DEF005 assembly
   fragility; DEF006 stock drift) and across ~20 named stocks — not an estimate of how often re-sequencing diverges
   in reality. The "0 clean" is an artifact of publication, not a measurement of zero clean re-sequencings.

4. **What the census can and cannot support:**
   - **Can support — existence:** the under-determination is *detectable in the published literature*, in multiple
     forms and across many virus families, and the field itself handles these as routine corrections ("technical
     noise"). This is exactly CNJ150.
   - **Cannot support — rate:** "X% of re-sequencings diverge," or "re-sequencing rarely reproduces." A rate needs
     a design that also observes the clean cases — (a) a prospective re-sequencing batch over a fixed sample of
     stocks with a count of divergent vs clean outcomes regardless of publishability (the wet analogue is the A1
     mock-sequence arm), or (b) a timed census of *consecutive* database deposits rather than a search over
     self-selected "correction" papers.

5. **Analogy.** Searching the literature for "re-sequencing divergence" is like estimating plane-crash frequency
   from news reports: every crash is reported, the thousands of uneventful landings are not. The census shows
   crashes *occur*, and *why*; it cannot give the crash *rate*.

**Bottom line for the CNJ150 upgrade decision:** the census upgrades CNJ150 from a bare Conjecture to a *supported
existence* claim (the ambiguity is found, published, and multi-form) but **not** to a *prevalence* claim. Any
Observation/Lemma wording should say "detectable," not "common" or "rare."

## The strong signal — same-strain intrastrain variation (searched 2026-11-14)

Beyond the 20 same-stock pairs, the literature holds a dedicated thread — mostly the herpesvirus-genomics
community (Szpara lab and collaborators) — showing that the deposited "reference genome" is a single haplotype
of a **mixed population**, and that re-sequencing "the same strain" yields **inconsistent consensus**. These
are the Ortigas-Vasquez-type cases, the *strongest* P3.v1 signal (item 1 of the prediction list in `../p3.md`):

**Textbook acknowledgment (DEF006 in the field's own words):** "Virus strains used in studies are often
passaged many times before they become 'reference' strains. This process results in alterations to the viral
genome that reflect adaptation to its artificial (cell culture) environment." (Elsevier *Virus Strain* topic
overview) — the field itself states that the "reference strain" is a cell-culture-adapted artifact.

**Reading:** the strongest P3.v1 signal is *not* the retractions (weak, exists in every field) — it is this
thread: **the deposited reference genome is one haplotype of a mixed, passage-drifted population, and
re-sequencing the same strain reproduces inconsistently.** That is DEF005 + DEF006 together, and it is
field-specific. Continue mining the Szpara-lab publication list.
