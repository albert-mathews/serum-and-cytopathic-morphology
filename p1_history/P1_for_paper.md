## 2. Narrative for the paper (Discussion subsection)

*Tone: process critique. Prefer Snippet **P1-S1** for the lock-in paragraph. Prefer “no serum added / serum omitted” over modern “serum-free medium (SFM)” (Snippet **P1-S2**).*

### Suggested subsection title

**Historical practice: enrichment change at the inoculation step**

### Body (copy-ready, ~350–450 words)

Standard Isolation protocols prescribe a richer medium for growing cell monolayers and a less enriched medium for viral inoculation and observation, then treat cytopathic effect (CPE) as evidence of virus. That dual-medium structure is not an incidental modern preference. It is visible in mid-century virus tissue-culture methods and was later written into diagnostic manuals.

Early operable systems often used a **single constrained fluid** through the culture life—for example balanced salt solution with ox serum ultrafiltrate rather than high whole-serum growth recipes—while CPE became a readable endpoint. Within a few years, laboratories explicitly **split** cell preparation from virus readout. Monkey kidney monolayers were grown with bovine or horse serum in synthetic medium 199, then washed or switched to medium **without added serum** for assay or infection, with authors citing inhibitory substances in serum. Continuous human lines grown in high concentrations of human serum were shifted to synthetic maintenance fluid after deliberate dilution of residual antibodies before poliovirus inoculation. By the late 1950s, “maintenance medium” was discussed as a distinct problem for virus work: animal sera could reduce sensitivity to viral effects, and comparative titrations showed large apparent inhibition of poliovirus when calf serum was present in maintenance fluid versus fluid with serum omitted. In at least one arbovirus assay system, high titers were reported without obvious CPE under serum-containing conditions, whereas **omitting serum** from the maintenance fluid yielded rapid cell destruction used as a titration endpoint—evidence that medium composition can change whether CPE is the readable signal, even when the experimental narrative remains virus-centered.

Diagnostic codification followed. By 1969, a standard tissue-culture chapter for viral diagnosis defined growth versus maintenance media, instructed removal of growth medium before virus inoculation, recommended washing when growth serum might contain inhibitors, and listed recipes that often used about **2% fetal bovine serum** (or no serum) for viral maintenance. Later institutional guides retain the same operational split (commonly growth near **10% FBS**, maintenance near **1–3% / 2% FBS**) with **heterogeneous** rationales—overgrowth control, inhibitors, attachment, or none stated. National and international laboratory manuals for measles surveillance (CDC, 2002; WHO EPI, 2007) state the same growth-versus-maintenance serum split in explicit isolation protocols.

Two points matter for interpretation. First, mid-century motives for changing medium at inoculation are framed as effects on **the virus** (antibodies, inhibitors, “sensitivity”), not as tests of whether enrichment change alone can produce CPE-like morphology in **uninoculated** cultures. Second, we did not find a foundational Isolation methods paper that systematically varies serum enrichment as the sole independent variable on matched uninfected monolayers to establish CPE as a virus-specific detector. Once CPE under Isolation conditions is taken as the operational marker of virus, medium effects that change CPE readability will tend to be explained as effects on the virus. That pattern is what the historical rationales show. It does not prove that no infectious agent exists; it shows that the inference from CPE to virus was insulated from medium as a confounded co-variable. Contemporary Isolation practice still co-varies inoculum and enrichment; whether that co-variation is audited is a separate empirical question (control design; serum-only morphology without inoculum).
